Systems that comprehend natural language, execute reasoning on intricate inquiries and reply in a sensible and contextually relevant written prose have come to signify a crucial aspect of the digital era: conversational AI systems. Large language models (LLMs) have shown great potential in revolutionizing the way humans access information, learn new skills, communicate over a distance, and interact with technologies in their daily lives; from their ability to comprehend vast amounts of data to their interaction with natural language processing. The large language models (LLMs) of today have revolutionized the ways in which people interact with information, gain new skills, communicate across great distances and, indeed, communicate with technology. The potential applications of such systems are unlimited: If someone in the world anywhere asks a question in his cell phone, he might get an intelligent and nuanced response within a couple of seconds.But that's a nice promise that comes with a big caveat: that it was offered to the English language and to a few high-resource languages [1]. AI training systems, test sets, standardisation plans and commercial interests are organised for linguistic communities which are represented electronically. These hundreds of millions of people who use languages that do not have such a digital footprint will only get a promise from conversational AI.One of the languages is Shona. It is the main language of Zimbabwe and is spoken by over 15 million Zimbabweans, accounting for over 70% of the total population [1]. Shona is not only a side road language, but the heart of an entire nation. At the time of this study, there is no generally available conversational AI system that can comprehend and react in a natural way in Shona. Meanwhile, the world buzzed with the latest in the advancement of artificial intelligence and the people of Shona, and its 15 million speakers were mostly unnoticed.This is a project that is consciously and directly addressing that lack: Ruzivo. Ruzivo translates to ‘knowledge or wisdom' in Shona language and is an appropriate name for a system that aims to bring knowledge to the Shona speaking communities, in their own language, on their own terms. Ruzivo is recommended as a text based conversational AI system, which can be used only in Shona, and can ingest text, document, upload files and document. This was based on the premise that Zimbabwe's population should be able to access AI tools that are offered in English-speaking countries and access should be an action that is technically informed, targeted and purposeful.The Ruzivo project has its foundations in the context presented in this chapter. The research methodology used in the research is Design Science Research (DSR) research methodology [5] that sees the research process as design and evaluation of artefacts. The chapter puts the study into the global and local context of AI and NLP, identifies the problem and the general purpose of the study and outlines four SMART objectives. It also sets limitations and delimitations, lists development instruments, provides the development work plan and basis for rationale of research.1.2 The background of the study is:1.2.1 The Digital Language Divide in Artificial IntelligenceThe term Artificial Intelligence (AI) refers to the ability of computers to think and learn like humans do. The term Artificial Intelligence (AI) describes how a computer can think and learn like humans.The world NLP market is very skewed towards the high resource languages and is well documented. In Joshi et al.'s recent work [1] it was revealed that majority of the digital resources available to train the best language models around the world were in English. Other local languages (including Swahili, Yoruba and Zulu/Shona) were ranked at the lowest level of resources in comparison to their high resource counterparts in terms of decades of lag in research, commercialisation and deployment of AI tools.This inequality has a great effect. The bias however is embedded into AIs that are trained in mostly high resource language data, knowledge is mostly in the high resource language and speakers of other languages are second-class. The term "linguistic digital exclusion" was coined by Nekoto et al. [2] to describe the phenomenon that the use of AI powered tools is not clearly expressed but is in fact limited by language. This was disputed by their participatory research project Masakhane, which sought to mobilize African researchers and communities for the development of MT models for the use of African languages, that demonstrated that meaningful progress could be made when the communities were involved in the research process.1.2.2 Shona Language is a component part of the Digital Age. Shona belongs to the Bantu branch of the Niger-Congo language family and is spoken in parts of Mozambique, Zambia and Zimbabwe [4]. It is the first language of about 70% of the people in Zimbabwe while a good chunk of the remaining users speak it as a second language. The voice of Shona, however, is a lot less prominent on the Internet. Less than adequate language communication and language resources for the Shona language, with the majority of the information provided through religious materials; with a few news websites in the Shona language (Kwayedza, ZBC Shona) and a few government publications. Some existing multilingual models such as mBERT [3] and XLM-R [9] partially added Shona to their training corpora but were not very effective on Shona without fine-tuning. Conneau et al. [9] showed that large-scale multilingual pre-training resulted in good cross-lingual representations for high resource languages, while truly low-resource languages, with very little web presence, gained very little. This has a direct impact on Shona's work on training multilingual models that are used in the mixture of languages.There are a number of parallel corpora containing Shona in the text domain. To create a broad-coverage parallel corpus in Shona, with over 300 languages, the Agić and Vulić [4] developed corpus of Jehovah's Witnesses publications (JW300 corpus) was used. A small subset of the CC-100 dataset was taken from Common Crawl web data and used as the Shona subset in the CC-100 dataset. Although these were helpful for the research of Shona NLP, they were insufficient for training a conversational language model and only contained a small number of sentences by today's standards for training a large language model (LLM).1.2.3 The gaps in research identified from the literature review are:The largest project in the African NLP field during the time of this research was the Masakhane project [2] which created machine translation models for over 38 African language pairs, and the participatory research model which involved the African communities at the center of the research. However, the focus of Masakhane was rather machine translation and not conversational AI, and the models they created were not packaged or incorporated into end-user applications that were made available to the general-savoring-Shoan users.A unified solution that can seamlessly integrate and easily access Shona's conversational AI and knowledge retrieval from a single platform was lacking. No one had attempted to create such components for one Shona AI system and use them in one mobile and Web application. Ruzivo is taking on this challenge head on.1.2.4 Motivating FactorsThere were two motivations for Ruzivo: technical and social. From a technical perspective, the project was designed to develop the first publicly available, conversational Shona AI system, with its user contributions consisting of a much-tuned LLM, curated data sets and a retrieval-augmented knowledge pipeline, all of which are openly licensed as a research artefact. These contributions were expected to be a foundation for future researchers to work with Shona NLP.On the social level, the stakes were even more significant. Zimbabwe's Vision 2030 national development agenda places digital transformation and economic inclusion at its heart. Yet a digital inclusion agenda that does not account for language is incomplete by design: if AI-powered tools are accessible only to those who can read and write in English, the majority of Zimbabweans — who conduct their daily lives in Shona — are structurally excluded from the digital economy. The participatory approach demonstrated by Nekoto et al. [2] confirmed that African NLP progress requires direct community involvement, a principle embedded in Ruzivo's community data collection methodology.1.3 Problem DefinitionDespite being spoken by over 15 million people comprising more than 70% of Zimbabwe's national population [4], the Shona language had no publicly available general-purpose conversational AI system and no foundational NLP infrastructure — a condition consistent with the lowest resource tier identified by Joshi et al. [1] — creating a digital exclusion gap that prevented Shona-speaking Zimbabweans from accessing AI-powered tools available to English-speaking populations, while simultaneously leaving the field of Shona computational linguistics without reusable research artefacts for future work.1.4 AimThe broad aim of this research was to design, develop, and deploy Ruzivo — a text-based conversational artificial intelligence system that operated exclusively in the Shona language, supporting text interaction and file uploads including documents — by fine-tuning a large language model on Shona language data, integrating a retrieval-augmented generation pipeline for knowledge grounding, and packaging all components into a publicly accessible hybrid application delivered as both a native mobile application and a web application, thereby establishing foundational Shona AI infrastructure and openly releasing all trained models and datasets for the benefit of future research and the nation of Zimbabwe.1.5 ObjectivesThe following four SMART objectives were derived directly from the core processes of the Ruzivo system.1.5.1 Objective 1 — Dataset ConstructionTo collect and curate a verified Shona text corpus of at least 90,000 sentences from publicly available sources by July 2026.1.5.2 Objective 2 — Language Model Fine-TuningTo fine-tune a large language model on the Shona corpus using QLoRA [6] by September 2026, achieving a minimum BLEU score improvement of 15% over the untrained baseline.1.5.3 Objective 3 — Knowledge Retrieval PipelineTo implement a FAISS-based retrieval-augmented generation pipeline [8] for context-grounded Shona responses, achieving at least 70% accuracy on factual queries in human evaluation by October 2026.1.5.4 Objective 4 — Application DeploymentTo develop and deploy a Shona-only text conversational application on Android, iOS, and web with document upload support by December 2026.1.6 LimitationsThe primary limitation of this research was data scarcity — a documented characteristic of low-resource language AI development [1], [2]. Available Shona text corpora were drawn from narrow domains — religious texts, Wikipedia, and news — with limited representation of natural conversational register. To address this, the training corpus was supplemented with filtered social media conversations and curated conversational instruction-response pairs.Web-scraped and web-crawled Shona text data contained noise, code-switching with English, and inconsistent orthography. Rigorous preprocessing including frequency-threshold English word detection was applied to mitigate this. GPU compute was limited to free-tier cloud platforms (Kaggle Notebooks), which extended training times and limited model scale experimentation. End-to-end text response latency on CPU-only inference hardware averages 60 to 90 seconds per response — a known constraint attributed to the absence of a dedicated GPU in the deployment environment.1.7 DelimitationsRuzivo was designed as a Shona-only text-based system. It did not accept input, process queries, or produce output in any language other than Shona. Users who submitted text in English or any other language received a Shona-language prompt instructing them to interact in Shona. This constraint was enforced at both the model training level and the application level and represented a deliberate design decision ensuring the system remained deeply optimised for its intended Shona-speaking audience.The hybrid application targeted Android and iOS via React Native Expo for the native mobile experience, and modern web browsers via Expo Web for the desktop experience. Native distribution through the Google Play Store or Apple App Store was out of scope. File upload support was scoped to PDF, Microsoft Word (.docx), and plain text (.txt) documents with a maximum file size of 10 MB. Within Shona, the system primarily targeted the Zezuru standard written dialect as most represented in available digital text data; full dialectal coverage across Ndau, Karanga, and Manyika varieties was deferred to future work. Voice input, voice output, and image understanding were identified as significant extensions to the system and are recommended as future work in Chapter 5. The system was designed for general-purpose conversational use and was not optimised for high-stakes domain-specific applications such as legal advice or clinical decision support.1.8 Development InstrumentsThe development of Ruzivo drew on a carefully selected stack of open-source and freely available hardware and software instruments. All training compute was sourced from free cloud GPU platforms. On the technical side, the project focused on the minimum prerequisite level of language AI development identified by Joshi et al. [1] which is text-based conversational language AI with a fine-tuned LLM, retrieval-augmented knowledge pipeline [8] and curation of datasets as openly licensed artefacts. The Design Science Research (DSR) methodology [5] instilled the incorporation of practical artefacts as well as theoretical contributions to the research, which added to the body of knowledge on the development of AI for African languages in a low resource environment.It was a well-motivated and correct decision to make Ruzivo a Shona-only system. The project had the advantage of limiting the system to Shona to create a much more optimised AI than a diluted multi-lingual experience. It has also avoided the error made by some of the multilingual chatbots, switching to English when they are unsure, which directly had thrown the principle at the heart of the system, the provision of services to Shona speakers in Shona.Justifications behind the hybrid application approach, which involved providing a mobile application and a web application from the same code base, were that most of the Internet traffic in Zimbabwe was through mobile devices and not desktop computers. React Native Expo boosted development efficiency, enabling both platforms to be developed from a single code base and serving a native user experience on mobile. Finally, the research delivered a proof of concept for an end-to-end pipeline for developing an end-to-end LRC AI system for the under-resourced African languages using only free tools, datasets, and cloud compute; which can be easily replicated in other communities in Africa.1.11 SummaryRuzivo, a conversational AI system that can interact with human in Shona language was introduced in this chapter. The background was a digital language gap for low resource African languages [1, 2] and also connected Ruzivo to being a pragmatic solution and also a research contribution. The formal problem was given as the compound absence of the existence of any Shona AI system and the resulting digital exclusion of more than 15 million Zimbabweans who speak Shona as their home language [4]. The research aim was to be defined and four SMART objectives were developed from the core system processes of the construction of the datasets [Obj. 1]. The language model fine-tuning [Obj. 2]. The implementation to use RAG pipeline will be discussed (Obj. 3]. In order to deploy the app as a full stack application it is necessary to do so [Obj. 4]. Expansion and limitation were specified, extensions of voice, image and multimodal, future work, were identified, work plan was defined for two academic semesters up until December 2026 and work tools were catalogued.Chapter 2 conducts a critical review of the existing literature through the funnel approach, starting from international context to local context, with the aim of pointing out the gaps that were to be addressed by Ruzivo, in the fields of low resource NLP, Multilingual language model fine-tuning, retrieval-augmented generation systems, and Shona computational linguistics.References[1] P.
