The review takes a funnel method from international developments, such as large language models, retrieval-augmented generation and sentence embedding models, to regional developments in Africa's natural language processing, and finally, to the context of Shona computational linguistics and the Zimbabwean digital landscape.A number of important principles are underlying this chapter. A Large Language Model (LLM) is a deep neural network model trained with huge amounts of text, which can predict and generate natural language. Parameter-Efficient Fine-Tuning (PEFT) are those techniques that apply the weights of a pre-trained model to a new task while only training a small percentage of its parameters; thereby, lowering compute and memory resources [7]. The idea of Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) [8] is to enrich the output of a language model by adding information from an external knowledge base during the generation process. A dense numerical representation of text into high dimensional space with similar texts close together is called a vector embedding [16]. The chapter points out the gaps tackled by Ruzivo and the proposed system displays with Feasibility analysis.2.2 Analysis of Study 2.2.1 International Level2.2.1.1 Large Language ModelsLarge language models have revolutionized the natural language processing industry. Brown et al. [10] presented an empirical study using GPT-3 showing that emergent few-shot learning is possible in LLMs scaled to $175B$ parameters, allowing a single LLM to be applied to a variety of different NLP tasks with few examples in-context of the task, and without any associated gradient updates. But GPT-4 and following could still only be used through rate-limited APIs, which made it a massive limitation for low-resource language research that required the expenses of training to be kept in check and model use behaviour fully transparent.At the same time, Touvron et al. [11] released a family of open-source language models, LLaMA 2, from 7 to 70 billion parameters, which demonstrated that it is possible to achieve performance results comparable to those of a proprietary model of similar size using an open-source model with a clearly defined training recipe. Each model was trained on an English-dominated data set, however, and inefficient tokenisation was witnessed in languages with low resources and morphologically rich, like Shona. Alibaba Cloud released open source-based Qwen2.5 family of 1.5 billion parameter model variants in 2024 with strong multilingual capabilities. The smaller number of parameters allowed it to be used with consumer hardware, even without GPU acceleration, which is crucial for the Ruzivo project since there is no dedicated GPU. As a result, it was decided that the language model for Ruzivo will be based on Qwen2.5-1.5B.2.2.1.2 Parameter-Efficient Fine-TuningMost academic researchers do not have the ability to do fine-tuning on hundreds or thousands of high-memory GPUs to update billions of parameters. Low Rank Adaptation (LoRA) [7] adds by the addition of trainable low rank decomposition matrices to the attention layers of a frozen pre-trained model, then trains only the added matrices, typically making up only 0.1% to 1% of the total parameters, resulting in comparable performance to full fine-tuning while consuming a fraction of the memory of full fine-tuning.Dettmers et al. [6] later used this method by adding Quantized LoRA (QLoRA), which quantises their Frozen BB into 4-bit after fitting LoRA adapters, making the GPU memory requirement significantly lower. In the Ruzivo project, QLoRA was used with Qwen2.5-15B using r=16 and α=32, to decompose the query, key, value, and output projection matrices in each attention layer. This setup had 1.548 billion parameters of which 4,358,144 were trainable, which took ~ 2 ½ hrs to complete the fine-tuning run on a single Kaggle T4 GPU, or 0.28% of the parameters trainable.2.2.1.3 Instruction Tuning and AlignmentFining-tuning a base LLM on raw text corpora does not necessarily make it an "instruction-tuned" LLM or give it the ability to maintain a conversational style of writing. The FLAN model family was shown by Wei et al. [13] to be much better at zero-shot generalisation to unseen tasks when trained with fine-tuning on a large variety of tasks using natural-language instructions. To achieve alignment of the model output to human preferences, Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF) has been introduced by Ouyang et al. [14], who created the InstructGPT model and later ChatGPT. In cases where human feedback is not available and a project has limited resources for languages with a low number of speakers, Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT) can be used as a viable alternative to RLHF, using carefully selected instruction-response pairs.Instruction tuning is known to suffer from training data quality issues. When only degeneration is applied, i.e. training templates that produce fixed output, despite the input content, systematic biases are created in the models made from the training, as the model learns to relate the input it has, not to the output, but to a distribution of outputs. The issue was identified and fixed in the construction of the Ruzivo training corpus, which was using an instruction template that generated some 33,000+ training examples with no instance of input placeholder.2.2.1.4 Retrieval-Augmented Generation and Vector Search.Retrieval-Augmented Generation [8] was introduced as a framework to alleviate the hallucination problem in generative models by grounding their generative output from external knowledge. A RAG pipeline introduces a dense representation of the query, retrieval from an indexed knowledge base, and then use of prepended passages to the language model's context window for generation. Such a capability enables the model to generate factual answers whenever it receives factual questions, without having to embed all knowledge of the world into the hidden layers' parameters — as it is for low-resource languages, where the pre-training corpus simply isn't large enough to capture a lot of domain knowledge.Efficiency of nearest-neighbour search over large vector indices is essential for the performance of RAGs. Johnson et al. [15] created FAISS: Facebook AI Similarity Search which is a library of highly optimised algorithms implementing dense vector similarity search at billion scale. For the Ruzivo project the following parameters were used for the FAISS IndexFlatIP: "exact inner product search over L2-normalised vectors (i.e., cosine similarity)" This vector dimension, 98,993 vectors in 768 dimensions, provided sub millisecond retrieval on CPU.The quality of a RAG pipeline is limited by the quality of embedding model. Reimers and Gurevych [16] showed that sentence embeddings generated by siamese fine-tuning BERT on the natural language inference task bear strong correlation with human judgements of sentence similarity on cosine similarity scores. Sentence-BERT, however, and the multilingual offspring of its type were trained and used mainly in the context of European languages that have a lot of resources. Without empirical evidence, Sentence-BERT is not suitable for Shona. Feng et al. [17] proposed a general model for learning a sentence embedding across multiple languages, namely Language-agnostic BERT Sentence Embedding (LaBSE), which is the empirically supported model for Shona semantic embedding, as it was trained on translation sets of 109 languages containing Shona (sn) as an explicit language.2.2.2 Regional Level (Africa)2.2.2.1 The Masakhane InitiativeThe project previously referred to by Nekoto et al. [2] called Masakhane was the first large scale participatory research project specifically for African languages working on NLP. The tens of researchers and community contributors around the continent made up of Masakhane are operating in a distributed global network to generate useful machine translation benchmarks and models for more than 38 language pairs, including African languages, indicating that useful NLP research could be conducted by and for African communities with minimal external infrastructure. The project also contributed to the creation of a human annotated, domain-specific named entity recognition dataset for 20 African languages, including Shona, annotated by native speakers in the domain of journalism: MasakhaNER 2.0 [18]. Building on this, MasakhaNEWS [19] extended this contribution with some topic classification data for articles in African languages and also made supervised fine-tuning resources for African NLP tasks available, which were missing up to now.2.2.2.2 Multilingual Models for African LanguagesVarious multilingual model families have been aimed at addressing the need for better African language coverage. The pre-trained black-box checkpoints, AfroXLMR and AfroBERT, are based on XLM-R [9] with domain-adaptive pre-training using African language text, with results showing that these checkpoints achieve considerably better results than the original checkpoint on African NLP benchmarks. The BLOOM model was developed by BigScience which combines multiple African languages to form its pre-training mixture, but showed marked results which were not uniform in the case of low resource languages in Africa such as Shona, where the quantity of pre-training data was low compared to language subsets from Europe. This observation further buttressed the primary rationale that motivated the design choice for the development of a language-specific model (rather than following the multilingual approach in the African languages context): the notion that a whole model could be optimized for the African target language, namely Shona.Ruzivo's finding was to opt for a modelling strategy for the African target language, Shona, instead of the multilingual approach, given the direct motivation of such a choice.2.2.2.3 African Text CorporaGoogle has created parallel multi-lingual text and speech data labelled FLEURS [20] for 102 languages, including Shona (sn_zw). The text transcription part of the Shona FLEURS subset provided a small number of high quality Shona sentences from Wikipedia that made up the Ruzivo training set, namely 3,781 sentences. They came from carefully transcribed speech, and thus were a useful quality anchor to include in a body of text sourced from a wide variety of sources, in and out of the web.2.2.2.4 Code-Mixing in African Language CorporaThe problem of code-mixing, i.e. the frequent alternation of two or more languages in the same speaker's output or intra-sentential code-mixing (shifting between languages within a single sentence) is found in very great numbers in NLP in African language instruction. Overall, the code mixing of Shona and English is widespread across digital texts, especially in the context of social media platforms within the context of Zimbabwe. In a data set of 30,174 Shona language social media messages, Masoka [21] documented that most of the messages had some English language and hence, only about 40% aligned to a strict definition were predominantly Shona. This finding has implications for the quality of web-crawled Shona corpora (whether used in Shona or otherwise): data collected from just the general Web without any language-specific filtering might contain a considerable amount of code-mixed text, it might therefore reduce the quality of the Shona model if not cleaned first.2.2.3 Local Level (Zimbabwe and Shona)2.2.3.1 Linguistic Characteristics of ShonaBantu, with which Shona belongs, is one of the many members of the Niger-Congo phylum, which is characterized by specific typological properties that are difficult to model computationally [4]. Its morphology is extremely agglutinative; it is modular and combines tense, aspect, and subject agreement (among other things) with the substrate of a verb word, underlined by the addition of prefixes and suffixes. This morphological richness results in a high type-to-token ratio; in other words, the same meaning as in English using different words, may be said in Shona using a single, morphologically complex word, an increase in the tokenisation level of such a word with routine tokenisation by the Byte-Pair Encoding method may result in a high token-to-type ratio if the tokeniser is not trained on Shona text.The Mupanda system hierarchises Shona nouns into eighteen classes which are all preceded by a different prefix, some of which are extended to verbs and adjectives, and form part of agreement inflections [22]. While they help ensure the grammatical coherence of individual sentences, the actual learning effort required is significant for sequence models trained on small amounts of data: the learning system needs to acquire agreement patterns for all the co-referent elements in the sentence as well as the noun class of each referent. In this research, the rule-based morphological analyser library shona_spacy [22] was used for noun class detection and verbal agreement parsing in the Shona language within the framework of spaCy, with the integration of this library not having been shown in training pipelines for LLM before.The Shona language has several mutually intelligible but dialectally distinct phonological and lexicological variant that are known as Zezuru, Karanga, Ndau and Manyika. They participated in a compromise process which has resulted in the codification of standard written Shona, chiShona chakabatanidzwa, which is employed in formal written texts, government publications and in religious works. The orthographic/sound variation found in Web-crawled Shona corpora that have been aggregated without any dialectal normalization can mislead the language model for generating output in the standard-register because some lexical items, when sounded out, exhibit lateral/rhotic changes between dialectal/l/ and standard/r/ forms.2.2.3.2 Available Shona DatasetsIn the past, there were already the following datasets of Shona texts that were available in the public domain. High orthographic consistency and limited coverage in the religious domain were found in the JW300 corpus [4] comprising 786,371 parallel sentences between Shona and English for a sample of Jehovah's Witnesses publications. The subset Shona in CC-100 is from the Common Crawl, a data source filtered by Conneau et al. [9] but that has been proven to contain a number of artifacts via quality audits of CC-100 for low-resource African languages, including code-mixing contamination, boilerplate content, and systematic misclassification contamination, as well as other factors. Appointly 82,707 sentences on encyclopedic topics were contributed by the Shona Wikipedia dump. The Shona Slang dataset [21] provided 30,174 social media messages that were inteded and labelled with the sentiment of the content, although as mentioned above, the English portion of these messages had to be filtered out as part of a Shona only training pipeline.2.2.3.3 Learning how to embed models in ShonaBefore the implementation of the Ruzivo project there was no research report showing the empirical results of publicly available sentence embedding models in Shona semantic similarity tasks. The paraphrase-multilingual-MiniLM-L12-v2 model from the Sentence Transformers library [16] has been trained from a dataset that includes 50 different language pairs, mostly European languages and is devoid of any Sub Saharan African language in its training set. This limitation was verified by empirical evaluation in the Ruzivo project: When the indexed sentences were queried for semantic similarity, and also cosine similarity was calculated, results were obtained over 0.85, which happened to be semantically irrelevant to the query, meaning that it was surface-level similarity rather than semantic similarity. To the contrary, some systems including LaBSE [17] explicitly include Shona in their training set of 109 languages, yielding much more coherent retrieval results with queries from the same set, and this makes it a suitable choice as an RAG embedding model for the Ruzivo RAG.2.2.3.4 Previous Shona AI SystemsTo date, when this research was being conducted, no general purpose conversing AI system which understands and responds in Shona that has been (openly) documented and/or deployed has been found. The only precedents in the area of NLP were narrowly defined tasks involving NLP. Using the Shona Slang dataset, DistilBERT has been fine-tuned as a hybrid intent-recognition chatbot for informal Shona as shown in Masoka [21] which has a positive success rate on its defined intent categories of 96.4% for intent classification, but results in an intent-limited bot that cannot interact in open-domain conversations. The machine translation models demonstrated by Masakhane translated English with Shona by not conversational AI, and vice versa [2]. To the best of our knowledge, no existing (open-source) system that integrates knowledge retrieval with ChatGPT Shona conversational AI and file understanding was called together in one user-facing application before this work.2.3 Gaps IdentifiedAnalysis of related work at an international, regional and local level identified the following gaps, which the Ruzivo project aims to fill:Problem 1: Lack of general- purpose conversational AI system in Shona. There has been no previous published work that had created an open domain conversational language model that could understand and generate natural Shona content on various topic domains. Most of the previous Shona NLP systems targeted specific applications or could only produce English language outputs regarding Shona content [2, 21].Gap 2: Appropriate embedding models for Shona semantic search. There had not been any published embedding model, specifically trained to be multi-lingual, suitable for embedding Shona data, making the use of off-the-shelf RAG pipeline components [12] - [16] useless for knowledge retrieval in Shona case unless proper model selection and empirical testing is done [17].Gap 3: There is no integrated Shona text AI platform. Despite some works being done on the use of individual NLP components in Shona or related languages, until now there had never been an application that combined conversational AI, knowledge retrieval and understanding of document content in one single, easily-accessed application in Shona language.Gap 4: Quality of training data to do instruction tuning on Shona. The fine-tuned quality of models can be compromised because of much English contamination/lots of code-mixed text in the web-crawled Shona corpora [21]. To the best of our knowledge no previous study quantified the contamination specifically related to fine tuning of LLM for the Shona language or has proposed a corpus-vocabulary-subtraction approach to language filtering calibrated for Shona false positives.Gap 5: Lack of standardized conversational Shona learning resources. All of the Shona text corpora were collected from formal written texts (Bible, Wikipedia, news) or specific social media intents. There was no existing collection of naturally collected Shona conversations for fine-tuning LLM's so that the fine-tuned models gave formal, non-conversative outputs that didn't match the register required by a chat assistant.2.4 Proposed WorkThe proposed Ruzivo is a Seendet system that only uses text as its dialogue medium and targets each of the identified gaps directly. This system is based on a highly optimized Qwen2.5-1.5B language model [12] and a FAISS backed RAG pipeline with the embedding model of the LaBSE [17] with a FastAPI backend and mobile/desktops app with React native. The training corpus was built from several data sources using rigorous explicit controls for English contamination, along with carefully selected pairs of conversational instruction–response set for conversational training and a carefully curated subset of context-augmented examples such that they conform to the format of the items used at inference time in the RAG pipeline, enabling consistency throughout training and deployment.2.4.1 Feasibility Analysis2.4.1.1 Technical FeasibilityAll the key enabling technologies developed for Ruzivo are open source and have been shown at similar scale in previous work. Dubbed the Qwen2.5-1.5B Base model [12], this version can be downloaded from Hugging Face Hub under an open licence. For fine-tuning, the QLoRA fine-tuning methodology [6] is supported by the PEFT and TRL libraries and there is active support. There are production-ready vector search libraries on cpu, such as FAISS [15] and sentence-transformers [16]. A fine-tuning run requires a maximum of 1000 training steps, after which accessing free-tier GPU compute from the Kaggle Notebooks (T4 GPU) in Kaggle Compute would be enough. FastAPI gives a production-ready Python web framework, whilst React Native Expo provides the cross-platform development from a single codebase.2.4.1.2 Economic FeasibilityThe Ruzivo project was conceived to be implemented using tools and compute readily available in the public domain, including no budget requirement for software licenses, no cloud compute limits. Model training done on Kaggle Notebooks for free. Hugging Face Hub free tier were used for model and dataset hosting. This is due to the specified backend deployment to Render's free tier. All of the software requirements, such as PyTorch, Transformers, PEFT, TRL, FAISS, FastAPI and React Native Expo, are open source and free and fair to use. This "no cost" infrastructure solution will increase the replicability and transferability of the project to other low-resource language communities.2.4.1.3 Social FeasibilitySocial need for Ruzivo is well known. Today, more than 15 million Shona speakers (accounting for over 70% of Zimbabwe's population) are not reached by the AI-powered conversational tools that are provided in their native language [1]. Digital inclusion and increased knowledge of AI are specifically included in Zimbabwe's national development blueprint, Vision 2030. By directly delivering AI capabilities in the language most Zimbabweans use in their daily lives, the Ruzivo system supports this agenda. The community engagement was done using structured instruments for data collection in Shona language which not only gave training data, but also gave a way for community engagement that was in harmony with the participatory research principles set by Masakhane initiative [2].2.4.1.4 Operational FeasibilityRuzivo is viable in terms of time and budgets as part of the project. The FastAPI's backend architecture uses a relational database (PostgreSQL), a cache (Redis), and the LLM and RAG components can each be loaded as a separate service for individual testing and validation that can be later integrated into the full system. It's lazy loading to get the health endpoint of the LLM service to return quickly, without waiting for the LLM to be loaded into memory. Minimizing the amount of overhead required to implement the behavior of the React Native Expo frontend on Android, iOS and web, given that it consists of a single codebase.2.4.1.5 Overview of Feasibility StudyTable 2. 1: Ruzivo Feasibility SummaryFeasibility DimensionAssessmentKey JustificationTechnicalFeasibleAll tools are open-source and validated; GPU compute available via Kaggle free tierEconomicFeasibleZero-cost tool stack; Kaggle, Hugging Face, and Render free tiers cover all compute and hostingSocialHighly Feasible15M+ Shona speakers with no existing AI tools; aligned with Zimbabwe Vision 2030OperationalFeasibleModular FastAPI architecture; staged deployment; lazy LLM loading; single codebase frontend2.5 SummaryExisting studies in the national, regional and local level were reviewed as related to the Ruzivo Project. At the international level, capabilities and limitations of large language models, parameter efficient fine-tuning methods such as LoRA [7] and QLoRA [6], and the RAG architecture [8] were explored, as well as sentence embedding models [16] and the FAISS vector search library [15]. For the purpose of supervised fine-tuning of conversational models, the instruction tuning methods used by FLAN [13] and InstructGPT [14] were discussed. The most relevant pre-existing African NLP efforts – such as the Masakhane initiative [2] and MasakhaNER 2.0 [18] or MasakhaNEWS [19] – as well as a ubiquitous phenomenon in African languages, namely code-mixing [21] (Masoka), were identified at the regional level. At the local level the analysis of Shona's agglutinative morphology, its noun class system Mupanda [22] (which presents a significant challenge for any Local Bilingual Information Service), dialectal variation and limitations in the availability of Shona material were discussed, while empirical evidence for the use of LaBSE [17] instead of MiniLM [16] for Shona semantic search was provided. Five gaps were identified—there was no conversational-AI for the Shona language; the embedding models were not suitable; there was no integrated text-AI platform; training data was poor quality; there was no training data that was curated conversational data. Ruzivo was offered as the proposed solution and proven to be feasible in all four dimensions.Chapter 3 introduces the Methodology, which involves information about the hardware and software setup, the layout of the architecture, the methods of data collection and data preprocessing, the design of algorithms, and the data from the training and validation of all the Ruzivo modules.References[10] T.
